# Dost: donor research and design decisions

Reviewed September 30, 2026. This is secondary research, a content audit, and a design evaluation. No donor interviews, surveys, A/B tests, or conversion measurements were conducted for Dost. Audience needs below are hypotheses to validate with actual supporters.

## Recommendation

Present Dost as an organization: lead with food assistance, medical support and education costs, then explain local coordination, annual reporting and leadership. Keep individual organizers’ personal lives and dinner preparation out of the institutional introduction. Community events and annual appeals belong further down the page. Let source-linked records and identified leadership support the organization’s credibility.

The design should make five questions easy to answer: What does Dost pay for? Who coordinates the work? What happened to last year's money? Who is responsible? How do I donate or ask a question?

## Likely visitors and tasks to validate

1. **A first-time visitor referred by a friend:** Understand the work, see who is involved, and establish whether to donate. The homepage now answers what, where, and how before asking the visitor to read a mission statement.
2. **A previous donor:** Find the relevant year and distinguish the amount raised from the money that supported families. The annual archive preserves the year of the work separately from the publication date.
3. **An iftar guest:** Recognize the family and the event, then reach the donation campaign. Past invitations retain explicit dates and past-event labels.
4. **A donor seeking documentation:** Find a receipt contact, source report, or organization documents. Contact and reporting links remain in the main navigation and footer.

These are task hypotheses derived from Dost's sources and this conversation, not personas discovered through interviews.

The homepage does not republish identifiable beneficiaries' medical histories. It describes the funded needs and links to the original report for readers seeking the full account. Photographs are existing organization assets; captions do not invent dates or locations more precise than those established.

## Institutional voice

Use the organization as the subject: “Dost supports families…” and “Our published reports…”. Describe what assistance covers and where it is coordinated. Keep names and roles in Leadership. The owner has asked to remove the earlier family-dinner narrative and personal-work-schedule language from the public introduction.

A professional voice does not require a claim of worldwide reach, a full-time field team, institutional partnerships or capacity to serve millions. None of those claims is established by the available record.

## Remaining credibility work

Design cannot replace organizational documentation. The site still needs the owner's confirmed legal name, EIN, public email, any public mailing address, and the documents they want published. Team roles are retained from the original website and should be confirmed. Contact delivery is unverified. Older private annual records are still needed, and the 2026 campaign has no completed distribution report in this archive. The report summaries are not audited financial statements.

Keep original reports available. Retain the caveats: 2021 is a plan, 2025 allocations are approximate, and 2018 and 2025 reports were published the following year. Missing years should not count toward an invented cumulative total.

## Next: a practical donor usability study

Recruit 5–6 people for an initial qualitative round: two past donors, two people unfamiliar with Dost, and one or two iftar participants. Include mobile users. This has not been done, and no participants have been contacted.

Use 20-minute sessions. Ask participants to think aloud without explaining the site first:

1. After looking at the homepage briefly, describe what Dost does and where it works.
2. Find what happened to the 2025 donations. Explain the difference between money raised and family support.
3. Find the 2021 record and say whether it is a plan or a completed distribution.
4. Find who runs Dost and describe how the organization fits into their lives.
5. Find how to ask for a receipt. Do not submit personal data.
6. Start the donation journey and stop before payment. Say which service will process the gift.

Record task completion, wrong turns, misunderstood amounts, and the participant's own words. Ask what evidence is still missing before asking whether they like the design. A useful internal target is that a new visitor can explain what and where within 20 seconds, and locate the latest financial summary in under a minute. These are proposed acceptance criteria, not measured results or research-derived conversion promises.

If multiple participants misunderstand the same point, revise it and run another small round. With low traffic, direct observation will usually give more actionable feedback than trying to declare a statistically significant A/B-test winner.


## Gallery and donation-focused revision

The primary objective is to give visitors enough evidence and confidence to donate. Visual scale does not establish operational scale. The site should look established, while avoiding unverified claims about reaching millions of people, invented endorsements, or a cumulative beneficiary count assembled from incompatible annual figures.

Additional primary research reviewed:

- [NN/g, Carousels on Mobile Devices](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-carousels/): partial adjacent items signal that more content exists. Sequential navigation limits discovery; keep the collection short, provide direct access, support swipe, and keep essential information available elsewhere.
- [Baymard, Always Use Thumbnails to Represent Additional Product Images](https://baymard.com/research-articles/always-use-thumbnails-additional-images): observed ecommerce users found thumbnail previews more informative than dots. Applying that finding to a nonprofit archive is an inference, not a tested donation effect.
- [W3C WAI, Carousels Tutorial](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/carousels/): preserve keyboard operation, communicate item changes, manage focus, and let people control movement. The implementation uses manual navigation and respects reduced-motion preferences.

| Before | After | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wide empty mat beside the selected image | Selected image in front, with the previous and next records visibly behind it | Makes the collection apparent and uses the horizontal space. |
| Different photographic colors and hard edges | Rounded frames and a light warm CSS treatment across photos and video | Visual consistency without replacing or altering the source files. |
| Caption without a date field | Year position directly below the media | Supports understanding of the archive; unknown dates remain explicitly unconfirmed. |
| A campaign total as the main financial feature | Work areas above the gallery; the latest completed distribution report in the reporting section | Centers the institution and its documented work. Current campaign amounts and supporter counts are omitted at the owner’s request. |
| Ambiguous contribution language | Clear Donate links to the existing Givebutter campaign | Keeps the donation destination easy to identify. |

The layered presentation is a visual design judgment. No research reviewed establishes that an Apple-style 3D layout or a warm hue will increase donations. Actual Dost usability sessions should check whether visitors can identify the year, distinguish fundraising from distribution, find the next item, and reach Donate without needing an explanation. Compare it with a simple image strip if the depth effect distracts from the records.

Additional reference pages reviewed on September 30, 2026:

This is a stronger presentation, but “highest trust” is not established by desk research or visual polish. The material gaps remain public organization identification, accessible organization documents, confirmed contact delivery, confirmed media dates and missing annual records. Actual donor usability sessions are still needed to assess whether this version answers visitors’ questions.

## Year-folder media workflow and visible reporting history

| Before | After | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gallery items required manual paths and dimensions | Year folders feed an importer that resizes photographs, converts videos, generates posters and adds dates | Makes future additions practical while preserving originals and image proportions. |
| Only the featured years were immediately visible | A linked 2018–2026 timeline distinguishes reports, updates, appeals and records still to add | Makes the length of the record visible without implying missing reports have already been published. |
| Five thumbnail columns assumed a small fixed gallery | A scrolling thumbnail strip and year selector accommodate additional media | Keeps the layout usable as the archive grows. |
| Report carousel copy was hard-coded | Featured cards and timeline use the same report records, including imported report PDFs | Adding the 2024 document updates its availability everywhere. |

The importer is local and does not publish automatically. Files are grouped by the owner's year folder rather than guessed from image contents. It preserves originals, corrects photo orientation, avoids upscaling and removes EXIF metadata from optimized images. Videos retain their aspect ratio and receive a native player and poster. Source PDFs are copied unchanged. Biography dialogs and matching team cards remain in place.
